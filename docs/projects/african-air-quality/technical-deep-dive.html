African Air Quality Analytics

Engineering a reproducible analytical pipeline from a heterogeneous African air-quality archive.

The main case study presents this project from an analytical and portfolio perspective. This deep dive documents how the dataset was reconstructed, validated, modeled, and analyzed, and why specific engineering decisions were made.

The central design principle was simple: do not make the data look cleaner than the evidence allows. Observations were preserved wherever possible, while structural validity, temporal continuity, analytical eligibility, provenance, and quality concerns were handled explicitly.

01 · System design

Project architecture

The project began with a public archive of low-cost air-quality observations from Sensors.AFRICA, accessed through the openAFRICA CKAN API. The objective was not a PM2.5 dashboard. It was a reproducible analytical system that could answer five progressively harder questions.

  1. What data actually exist in the archive?
  2. Can those observations be reconstructed into a coherent canonical dataset?
  3. Which sensor histories are suitable for which analytical tasks?
  4. What should happen when observations appear anomalous or extreme?
  5. What statistical and forecasting conclusions can legitimately be supported by the resulting population?

03 · QUALIFY

04 · ANALYZE
Model and report

Snowflake, dbt, R, and Power BI for reproducible analysis and communication.

The archive audit catalogued 95 datasets and 641 resources, and 324 resources associated with the target-city investigation were downloaded and audited. The archive carries PM1, PM2.5, PM10, temperature, and humidity measurements; Version 1 deliberately focuses on PM2.5.

The analytical city population is Nairobi, Nakuru, Accra, Abuja, Lagos, Kano, Kumasi, and Awka. Ado-Ekiti, Akure, and Lusaka were encountered during source reconstruction and retained as provenance context only. They were not carried into the analytical population, which is a scope decision rather than a failed quality test, and the eight-city population is not a ranking of cities by data quality or importance.

02 · Source reconstruction

Structural validity comes before analytical eligibility

The archive was not treated as a collection of clean CSV files. Resource-level identity was retained, and parsing and structural validation were separated from every later analytical decision.

A naive pipeline would have been download, concatenate, clean, aggregate, chart. That sequence cannot answer where an observation came from, which resources contributed to an hour, or how much of the archive was excluded.

Reconciliation of PM2.5 observations

Category Observations
Raw PM2.5 observations 28,374,186
Out of analytical scope 24,229
In scope but structurally invalid 432
Structurally valid and in scope 28,349,525

The 432 invalid observations were 144 nonnumeric values and 288 negative values. Zero readings were retained rather than treated as automatically invalid. The reconciliation closes exactly: 28,349,525 + 24,229 + 432 = 28,374,186. Out-of-scope and structurally invalid are different classifications, and neither was a silent cleaning step.

Provenance as part of the data model

city→sensor→location→source resource→source file→timestamp→observation

A canonical hourly record can aggregate observations from several source resources, so RESOURCE_IDS and SOURCE_FILES are deliberately plural. A resource identifier is not a sensor identifier: one sensor can contribute through multiple files, and one canonical hour can draw on multiple resources.

Retaining that lineage is what later made it possible to trace an extreme reading back to its source material instead of deleting it.

Why the hourly value is weighted

Where several observations contributed to one hour, the hourly mean was rebuilt from observation sums and counts rather than by averaging resource-level means.

hourly mean = (sum of PM2.5 sums across resources) ÷ (sum of observation counts across resources)

This preserves observation weighting. An unweighted average of resource means would give a resource containing one reading the same influence as one containing hundreds. It also matters later: the 1,000 µg/m³ QC threshold is applied to this hourly mean.

The canonical output keeps minimum and maximum PM2.5, observation counts, negative, zero, and high-value counts, exact-duplicate counts, resource counts, and source identifiers, so that each hourly value stays connected to the evidence behind it. Hourly aggregation does not imply that all sensors sample identically.

04 · Eligibility contract

Eligibility is not structural validity

A structurally valid observation can belong to a short or interrupted history. The project separates four questions that a single "valid" flag would blur.

Structural validity

Can the observation be parsed and does it satisfy basic rules?

Rule A eligibility

Does the sensor/location history contain a qualifying continuous segment?

Temporal validity

Does the row fall in a known temporal anomaly?

Modeling eligibility

Does the row belong to the final forecasting population?

Rule A

A segment is eligible when it contains a continuous run of at least 24 hours and at least 24 usable forecasting rows. Usable rows are the observed hours in a segment that remain after subtracting hours flagged by two screens: PM2.5 above 1,000 µg/m³, and an absolute robust z-score above 6 (computed from the sensor-level median and median absolute deviation, and only where the deviation is positive). A third screen, observations above the sensor-specific 99.9th percentile, is a diagnostic flag for inspection and is not deducted from the usable-hour count. The two Rule A thresholds are evaluated separately, and these are project screening rules, not universal physical limits or proof of sensor failure.

Rule A is a minimum-history contract, not a verdict on data quality and not a claim that an eligible segment suffices for every model.

Temporal validity and the modeling population

A row can satisfy every structural and eligibility rule and still sit inside a known anomalous period. The documented case is Kano, where four segments (sensors 4990/4016 and 4995/4017) carry corrupted epoch timestamps from 1970 and 2000. A blanket rule such as timestamp >= 2020 would have removed them, but it would also have imposed an arbitrary cutoff on heterogeneous source histories. The anomaly was instead characterized explicitly in an intermediate dbt model, INT_KNOWN_TEMPORAL_ANOMALIES, and each row receives a TEMPORAL_VALIDITY_STATUS of NORMAL or KNOWN_EPOCH_ANOMALY.

06 · Quality control

Flag, retain, investigate

A large PM2.5 value can be a genuine episode, sensor-specific behavior, a fault, or a source problem. Deleting it would remove exactly the evidence needed to tell which.

hourly PM2.5 mean > 1,000 µg/m³ → PM25_ABOVE_SENSOR_RANGE = TRUE

This is an adopted analytical screening threshold. It is not a universal health limit and not a claim that 1,000 µg/m³ is the physical maximum of every sensor. The field name reads more strongly than the rule: it marks a review trigger, not a demonstrated sensor-range violation.

07 · Spatial analysis

Nairobi: proximity is not equivalence

The engineering layer covers eight cities. The deeper spatial, anomaly, and forecasting work focuses on Nairobi, and that was a methodological choice, not a geographic one.

Nairobi holds the densest sensor network in the archive: 389,721 of the 641,310 modeling rows and 128 of the 183 modeling-eligible series. Several other cities have only a handful of sufficiently continuous series, so the same analysis there would have rested on substantially less robust evidence. Rather than force identical depth everywhere, the detailed work went where the temporal and spatial structure could support it.

Coordinates are evidence, not fixed truth

A durable lookup was built at metadata/sensor_location_coordinates.csv: 3,417 coordinate records, 129 unique locations, and 323 unique sensors. Five sensor/location combinations and four location IDs have more than one coordinate pair, so a location ID cannot be assumed to be a fixed point. The lookup was kept separate from the PM2.5 fact table instead of forcing a join on non-unique keys.

Which pairs can be compared?

Overlap in the broad window does not mean two locations ever reported in the same hour, so exact simultaneous observations were counted separately: 74 pairs had any, 62 had at least 168 paired hours, and 46 had at least 720.

A concrete example: locations 3966 and 3967

The two locations are about 0.6 km apart and share the same pms5003 sensor-type label. They have 4,166 simultaneous hourly observations, yet their Pearson correlation is approximately 0.002.

A near-zero Pearson correlation means no detectable linear relationship between paired hourly readings. It does not rule out nonlinear or lagged association, and Pearson is sensitive to outliers: both of these sensors contain readings above 3,000 µg/m³, and the inventory in section 10 lists 17 flagged hourly rows for sensor 4900 at location 3967, so a rank-based or flagged-hours-excluded check would be a useful robustness test that was not run in Version 1. Location 3967 also has a median of about 0.3 µg/m³ against 30.7 for 3966, so part of the divergence may reflect that sensor's behavior and not purely local air quality.

Interpretation

Geographic proximity alone is insufficient to establish comparable hourly PM2.5 behavior.

The comparison does not show the two locations experienced independent ambient conditions. Differences could reflect local exposure, sensor behavior, data-quality problems, or a mixture.

08 · Anomaly investigation

Investigating Nairobi's extreme observations

The question was whether extreme values reflected a shared pollution event, or were concentrated in particular sensor histories and source resources.

Four locations were examined: 3573 as a clean comparison location, 3626 for its prolonged extreme regime, and 3966 and 3967 for isolated extremes and their proximity. Two thresholds serve different purposes. Observations above 100 µg/m³ define exploratory extreme episodes; the broader QC flag fires above 1,000 µg/m³. A reading above 100 is not automatically QC-flagged.

The mean at 3626 is dominated by its extreme regime, which is why medians and upper-tail measures describe it better.

An extreme episode is a contiguous run of observations above 100 µg/m³, split whenever the gap exceeds one hour, so the episode count depends on that declared rule and is not a count of distinct physical events. Across the three locations that exceed the threshold, there are 471 extreme observations in 43 episodes, associated with 13 source resources. Five episodes and 15 peak observations were selected by script logic for detailed examination; they are illustrative, not a statistical sample.

Resource concentration

Every one of the 471 observations retained a provenance path to its source resource and file, and all 13 resources matched the resource audit. Two resources account for 438 observations (92.99%) and five account for 458 (97.24%). All 13 resources also contain other canonical series, and nine contain another non-target series above 100 µg/m³. Concentration is a clue, not proof of a faulty resource, and resource-level co-occurrence does not show that different series recorded the same physical event.

A comparison is usable when another non-target sensor/location has an observation with the same source resource and the exact same hour as the target observation. All 466 target timestamps had another series in the same source resource; 463 (99.36%) had usable comparison data and 3 had none. The comparison returned 1,841 rows, of which 20 were above 100 µg/m³ and 116 were at or above 50 µg/m³. Eleven of the 463 target timestamps with usable comparison data had at least one other series recording PM2.5 above 100 µg/m³ (the 20 is a count of comparison rows; the 11 is a count of target timestamps). The test covers the same source resources only; it does not mean no other Nairobi sensor was operating at those hours.

These results show that extreme readings were not uniformly shared across co-recorded series, but they do not establish that every unshared extreme was erroneous or identify its cause. They also do not establish that no city-wide pollution event occurred. The stronger evidence is the temporal pattern at 3626.

Location 3626: a recurring extreme level after dropouts

The extreme-episode analysis for location 3626 focuses on the period from 2024-08-07 16:00 to 2024-10-12 00:00 UTC, which contains two multi-week gaps (the sensor's full history is longer and is used separately for forecasting): about 583 hours (11 August to 4 September) and about 408 hours (15 September to 2 October).

  • First regime, 4–15 September: the series returned from the first gap already near 3,330 µg/m³. The episode algorithm split this regime into five contiguous episodes of 34, 11, 107, 53, and 55 hours, totalling 260 extreme hours. Each episode begins exactly two hours after the previous one ends (one missing hourly slot), so the split into five follows from the rule that a gap greater than one hour starts a new episode; the intervals show no data loss beyond those single missing hours, and in effect this is one near-continuous regime. The 107-hour episode (6 September 17:00 to 11 September 03:00) peaked at 3,346.3 with a median near 3,332.
  • Second regime, 2–8 October: after the second gap the series returned near the same level for a single 150-hour episode (2 October 17:00 to 8 October 22:00), with a maximum of 3,336.9 and a median near 3,331.9. It then returned toward normal values, and the data end on 12 October.

The two regimes are separate, each beginning immediately after a genuine multi-week data gap, not one continuous event. The same near-identical level reappearing after independent dropouts is consistent with a recurring sensor or data-quality fault, and it is not what a city-wide pollution signal would look like. The mechanism could not be established: there was no firmware history, device diagnostic, or reference-instrument comparison. The finding is therefore treated as a data-quality investigation, not as evidence of ambient concentrations near 3,330 µg/m³, and the observations remain in the dataset.

09 · Forecasting experiment

A chronological baseline comparison

Does a lagged linear model improve next-hour PM2.5 forecasts over persistence?

The population comes from FCT_PM25_FORECASTING_POPULATION, extracted by R/scripts/05_modeling_extract.R for Nairobi: 389,721 rows across 128 sensor/location series. Each series is sorted chronologically, segmented wherever a gap exceeds one hour, and reduced to its longest continuous segment. That avoids stitching discontinuous periods into one sequence, but it also means the evaluation does not use every row; 389,721 is the extracted population, not the number of rows entering the folds.

Model and baseline

The baseline is persistence: the next-hour prediction equals the current value. It is a deliberately strong baseline for a short horizon. The model is a linear regression on past information only.

PM25_MEAN ~ lag1 + lag2 + lag3 + lag6 + lag24
          + roll_mean_24h + hour_of_day + day_of_week

Validation

A random split would let later observations inform the model evaluated on earlier ones. The experiment instead uses five-fold expanding-window walk-forward validation: the training window grows with each fold and the next chronological block is the test set. Each test block is 15% of the series' modeling rows (floored), so the five blocks together cover about 75% of each series and the first training window is only about the first 25%. That is a test-heavy design: early folds are fitted on short histories, which may understate what the model could achieve with more training data. A series is marked insufficient if its longest continuous segment has fewer than 200 rows, or if fewer than 200 complete rows remain after lag and rolling features are built. The 200-row minimum is a practical implementation threshold, not an optimized sample-size guarantee.

Headline result

Across the 60 Nairobi sensor/location series with sufficient continuous history, the lagged linear-regression model reduced mean walk-forward RMSE by 3.68% relative to persistence, with substantial heterogeneity across series.

The 3.68% is the arithmetic mean of per-series percentage improvements. It is not a pooled RMSE improvement and is not weighted by series length.

Examples of the spread include sensor 4849 at location 3626 (+16.3%), sensor 4898 at location 3966 (+11.6% as originally evaluated; see section 10), sensor 792 at location 3981 (+4.4% as originally evaluated; see section 10), and, on the negative side, sensor 53 at location 28 (−43.5%), sensor 128 at location 68 (−20.4%), and sensor 55 at location 30 (−14.5%). A simple temporal model gives modest average gains for most evaluable series, but its usefulness is strongly series-dependent. This is not evidence of uniform superiority and does not extend automatically to the other 68 series. Three examples were checked against QC flags (section 10): the 3626 result is unaffected, while the 3966 and 3981 results are sensitive to how flagged observations are treated.

What this establishes, and what it does not

The forecasting extraction does not filter on the QC flag, so these rows were not removed by it. The initial audit matched flags to the 60 series, not to the exact longest continuous segment used in each fold, so a follow-up audit checked all eight flagged pairs against their selected segments and chronological folds. For sensor 4849 (location 3626), the selected segment (523 rows, 499 modeling rows) begins in January 2024, before the September and October extreme episodes, and contains no flagged rows; the 410 flagged observations for that series lie outside it, so the +16.3% result is not directly affected. For sensor 4898 (location 3966), one flagged observation lies inside the segment. It is a test target in fold 1 and appears in the prior-24-hour history of 24 further test rows in that fold (25 affected test rows, but one flagged observation), and it is part of the training data in folds 2 to 5. The +11.6% result for that series therefore needed a sensitivity check, reported below. Sensor 792 at location 3981 also has one flagged observation in its segment, as a test target in fold 2. Sensor 4930 at location 3981 has none. Nor does the flag prove the values are wrong.

Sensitivity to flagged observations

Two series with a flagged observation inside their evaluated segment (4898 / 3966 and 792 / 3981) were examined in three separate ways, so that a change in the test population is not confused with a change in model training or in RMSE aggregation: the baseline evaluation reported in section 9, an evaluation-population sensitivity, and a training-population sensitivity.

Evaluation-population sensitivity

Pooled out-of-fold RMSE, weighted by the number of retained test observations. The restricted population drops test rows whose target or preceding 24 hourly records carry a sensor-range flag. Both models are scored on the same rows in each case.

Sensor / location Evaluation population Test rows Persistence RMSE Regression RMSE Regression vs persistence
4898 / 3966 Unrestricted 1,850 107.804 90.661 +15.90%
4898 / 3966 Restricted 1,825 14.074 14.435 −2.56%
792 / 3981 Unrestricted 1,365 245.236 224.928 +8.28%
792 / 3981 Restricted 1,340 6.859 11.574 −68.74%

Improvement is 100 × (RMSE persistence − RMSE regression) / RMSE persistence; positive values favour the regression. The two sources of change can be read apart. The original evaluation (section 9) averaged the five fold-level RMSEs without weighting (mean persistence RMSE 58.60 and 115.05; mean regression RMSE 51.79 and 109.97), giving +11.6% and +4.4%. Using the same unrestricted test population and walk-forward fold structure, switching from the unweighted mean of fold-level RMSEs to pooled RMSEs changes the improvement from +11.6% to +15.90% for sensor 4898 / location 3966, and from +4.4% to +8.28% for sensor 792 / location 3981: about four points from aggregation alone. The original and sensitivity scripts use the same fold construction, training rows, eight-predictor regression and persistence baseline; individual predictions were not saved and compared row by row, so the equivalence rests on the matching procedure. Removing 25 rows from each series (1.4% and 1.8% of test rows) moves them to −2.56% and −68.74%, a far larger change. The evaluation results are therefore highly sensitive to a small subset of test rows: persistence RMSE falls from 107.8 to 14.1 in one series and from 245.2 to 6.9 in the other. On the restricted population the regression does not beat persistence in either series, so the original advantage does not hold there. Because the step from the original to the restricted figures changes both the aggregation and the test population, the restricted figures are not a direct correction of the original results.

Training-population sensitivity

The regression was then retrained using only rows with no sensor-range flag in the target or the preceding 24 hourly records, and scored on the restricted population.

Sensor / location Persistence RMSE Original regression RMSE Restricted-training RMSE Original vs persistence Restricted-training vs persistence
4898 / 3966 14.074 14.435 12.47 −2.6% +11.4%
792 / 3981 6.859 11.574 6.25 −68.7% +8.9%

Restricting training to unflagged rows turns both series into gains on the restricted population. The +11.4% for location 3966 is close to the baseline +11.6% but is a different quantity (a different model on a different test population) and does not confirm it.

These results show that the measured forecasting performance of these two series depends on how flagged observations are treated. They do not show that the flagged measurements are erroneous. One limit remains: the restricted regression still uses lag features computed from the original series, so a flagged observation can influence later predictors even when the training target is eligible. This is a training-row sensitivity test, not a rebuild of the series with flagged observations removed from all feature calculations.

Evidence boundary

The 3.68% mean and 5.15% median improvements across the 60 series are baseline results from the original implementation, before this sensitivity analysis, and have not been recomputed under any QC treatment. Two series are not enough to recompute the aggregate consistently. Because both tested series were sensitive, the aggregate should not be described as robust to QC flags, and how much it would change is unknown.

12 · Interpretation boundaries

What Version 1 does not claim

The workflow improves traceability and reproducibility. It cannot resolve uncertainties the source archive does not contain enough evidence to settle.

  • That every sensor measurement is physically accurate.
  • That every extreme value is a hardware failure.
  • That nearby sensors measure the same ambient concentration.
  • That Nairobi results generalize to every city in the archive.
  • That the forecasting model is operationally superior, or that 3.68% applies to every series.
  • That the 1,000 µg/m³ threshold is a universal physical or health boundary.

Limitations

Heterogeneous archive

Resources differ in format, completeness, and measurement behavior. Provenance cannot recover metadata that was never supplied.

Sensor non-equivalence

Sensor labels do not establish calibration equivalence, and some PM2.5-labelled readings come from non-PM2.5 device types.

Coordinate ambiguity

Some sensor/location combinations have multiple coordinate pairs, so a mapped point does not prove a fixed deployment.

Limited corroboration test

The exact-hour comparison covered the same source resources only and did not test every Nairobi sensor. Eleven of the 463 timestamps with usable comparison data had another series above 100 µg/m³; the comparison does not establish the cause of any unshared extreme.

Pearson sensitivity

The 3966/3967 correlation includes extreme readings and was not re-run with a rank-based or outlier-excluded measure.

Unresolved mechanism

The 3626 pattern is consistent with a recurring sensor or data-quality fault; its cause cannot be established from these data.

Forecasting coverage

Only 60 of 128 Nairobi series were evaluable, each on its longest continuous segment, so results describe a selected subset of the available history.

Fold design

Test blocks cover about 75% of each series, so early folds train on short histories. Results may be sensitive to fold size, which was not varied.

QC overlap

Eight Nairobi pairs carry flagged modeling rows and all were audited. Four could not be evaluated for forecasting, two evaluated series have no flagged rows in their segments, and two (3966 and 3981) overlap the folds and were sensitive. The 60-series aggregate was not recomputed, and restricted training still uses lag features from the original series.

Model scope

Persistence versus a lagged linear model on PM2.5 and calendar features, with no meteorological inputs and no causal interpretation.

13 · Next iteration

What I would do differently in Version 2

Prioritize improvements that address demonstrated limitations, not model complexity for its own sake.

1 · Extend the QC sensitivity analysis

Recompute the 60-series aggregate under a documented treatment of flagged rows, rebuild lag features with flagged observations handled throughout, and test any remaining flagged series.

2 · Rank-based robustness

Add a rank-based or outlier-excluded correlation check for sensor pairs with extreme readings, such as locations 3966 and 3967.

3 · Meteorological covariates

Temperature and humidity were catalogued during source reconstruction but excluded from V1. They are a natural test of whether environmental variables improve next-hour forecasts.

4 · Nonlinear models

Compare nonlinear approaches under the same chronological validation to see whether the modest linear gain reflects real structure.

5 · Sensor metadata

Capture device types, deployment changes, coordinate uncertainty, and calibration information, including sensor-type-specific behavior.

6 · Cautious geographic extension

Extend the spatial framework to other cities only where overlap and comparability support the inference.

The principle stays the same: the design should grow because the data support a stronger question, not because a more complex model is available.

14 · Conclusion

A defensible path, not a claim of perfect data

The most significant output is the analytical infrastructure underneath the dashboard and the forecast.

The project reconstructs a heterogeneous archive into a canonical hourly dataset, reconciles every structural exclusion, separates eligibility from temporal validity, implements the analytical layer in Snowflake and dbt, and uses R and Power BI to investigate spatial differences, extreme observations, and forecasting performance. At each stage the pipeline keeps enough information to answer two questions: where did this number come from, and why was it considered appropriate for this analysis?

The forecasting result is modest and uneven: a simple lag model beat persistence for most evaluable Nairobi series, not all, and the QC sensitivity analysis shows that results for flagged series depend on how flagged observations are treated. The system does not eliminate uncertainty from heterogeneous public sensor data. It makes that uncertainty visible, traceable, and analytically manageable.

Central lesson

Good analytics begins with knowing exactly what the data represents, what it does not represent, and what evidence is required before making a stronger claim.
